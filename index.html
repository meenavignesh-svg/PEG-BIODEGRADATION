BioHub PEG Biodegradation

Problem: Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is widely used in pharma, cosmetics and industry. Higher-molecular-weight PEG can persist longer in water and soil before microbes fully break it down.

Our idea: Hold a working microbial team on a biochar–hydrogel support so contact time, local enzyme activity and process stability improve — then walk the waste through a clear five-step path to a treated stream.

PEG in the environment

PEG is water-soluble and used in many products. Lower MW grades break down more readily; higher MW material often needs chain shortening before full microbial use. Standard tests (e.g. OECD-style) show PEG can reach high biodegradation under the right inoculum and conditions.

Why immobilize?

Biochar offers surface area and a mild protective niche. Hydrogel holds water and cells in place. Together they aim for better retention, longer contact, and more stable operation than free-floating cells alone — a common pattern in enzyme and cell immobilization work.

Consortium logic

Roles are split on purpose: one face on polymer attack, one on smaller intermediates, one on biofilm and enzymes. Real PEG literature often cites Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas specialists; here the three strains illustrate a team design for the demo. Lab validation would fix the exact species and kinetics.

Honest scope

This page is a systems story for SIH judges — not a pilot plant or a claim of measured % removal. Next steps in a real project: bench tests, MW tracking, toxicity checks, and scale-up design.
